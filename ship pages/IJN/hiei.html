Imperial Japanese Navy

HIEI

Battlecruiser

Brief History Summary

Hiei was a warship of the Imperial Japanese Navy during World War I and World War II. Designed by British naval architect George Thurston, she was the second launched of four Kongō-class battlecruisers, among the most heavily armed ships in any navy when built. She was laid down in 1911 at the Yokosuka Naval Arsenal and formally commissioned in 1914. Hiei patrolled off the Chinese coast on several occasions during World War I, and helped with rescue efforts following the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake.

Starting in 1929, Hiei was converted to a gunnery training ship to avoid being scrapped under the terms of the Washington Naval Treaty. She served as Emperor Hirohito's transport in the mid-1930s. After the treaty fell apart in 1937, she underwent a full-scale reconstruction that completely rebuilt her superstructure, upgraded her powerplant, and equipped her with launch catapults for floatplanes. Now fast enough to keep up with Japan's growing carrier fleet, she was reclassified as a fast battleship. On the eve of America's entry into World War II, she sailed as part of Vice-Admiral Chuichi Nagumo's Combined Fleet, escorting the six carriers that attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941.

As part of the Third Battleship Division, Hiei took part in many of the Imperial Japanese Navy's early actions in 1942, supporting the invasion of the Dutch East Indies (now Indonesia) as well as the Indian Ocean raid of April 1942. During the Battle of Midway, she sailed in the Invasion Force under Admiral Nobutake Kondō, before being sent to the Solomon Islands during the Battle of Guadalcanal. She escorted Japanese carrier forces during the battles of the Eastern Solomons and Santa Cruz Islands, then sailed as part of a bombardment force under Admiral Kondō during the Naval Battle of Guadalcanal. In the early hours of 13 November 1942, Hiei engaged American cruisers and destroyers alongside her sister ship Kirishima. After helping to sink the light cruiser USS Atlanta and the destroyer USS Monssen, damaging numerous other warships, and helping kill two admirals, Hiei was crippled by shell hits from the heavy cruiser USS San Francisco that jammed her rudder. Subjected to a daylight air attack from Henderson Field and the aircraft carrier USS Enterprise, she was scuttled on the evening of 13 November 1942.

Design and Construction

Hiei was the second of the Imperial Japanese Navy's Kongō-class battlecruisers, a line of capital ships designed by the British naval architect George Thurston. The class was ordered in 1910 under Japan's Emergency Naval Expansion Bill, after Britain commissioned HMS Invincible in 1908. The four Kongō-class battlecruisers were built to match the naval capabilities of other major powers at the time, and have been called battlecruiser versions of the British (formerly Turkish) battleship HMS Erin. With their heavy armament and armor protection, the latter making up about 23.3% of their roughly 30,000-ton displacement, Hiei and her sister ships were far superior to any other Japanese capital ship afloat at the time.

Hiei's keel was laid down at Yokosuka Naval Arsenal on 4 November 1911, with most of the parts used in her construction made in Britain. She was launched on 21 November 1912, and fitting-out began in December 1913. On 15 December 1913, Captain Shichitaro Takagi was assigned as her chief equipping officer. She was completed on 4 August 1914.

Armament

Hiei's main battery consisted of eight 14-inch (36 cm) heavy-caliber guns in four twin turrets, two forward and two aft. The US Office of Naval Intelligence noted the turrets were similar to British 15-inch turrets, with improvements in flash-tightness. Each main gun could fire high-explosive or armor-piercing shells about 38,770 yards (19.14 nautical miles, or 35.45 km), at a rate of roughly two shells per minute. Following Japan's approach of building more powerful ships before rival navies did, Hiei and her sister ships were the first in the world to carry 14-inch guns. Each gun carried enough ammunition for 90 shots and had a barrel life of about 250 to 280 shots.

Her secondary battery originally included sixteen 6-inch (15 cm) 50-caliber guns mounted singly amidships, eight 3-inch (7.6 cm) guns, and eight submerged 21-inch (53 cm) torpedo tubes. The 6-inch guns could fire five to six rounds per minute and had a barrel life of about 500 rounds. They could fire both anti-aircraft and anti-ship shells, though their placement on the ship made anti-aircraft use mostly impractical. The eight 5-inch (40-caliber) guns added later could fire 8 to 14 rounds per minute, with a barrel life of 800 to 1,500 rounds. These 5-inch guns had the widest range of ammunition types: anti-aircraft, anti-ship, and illumination shells. Hiei also carried a large number of 1-inch (2.5 cm) Type 96 anti-aircraft autocannons.

Service

1914–1929: Service as a Battlecruiser

Hiei was formally commissioned on 4 August 1914 and assigned to the Sasebo Naval District, before joining the Third Battleship Division of the First Fleet two weeks later. On 23 August 1914, Japan declared war on Germany, occupying former German colonies in Palau and the Caroline, Marshall, and Mariana Islands. In October 1914, Hiei left Sasebo alongside Kongō to support Imperial Japanese Army units at the Siege of Tsingtao, but was recalled on 17 October. On 3 October 1915, Hiei and Kongō took part in sinking the target ship Imperator Nikolai I, a Russian pre-dreadnought captured in 1905 during the Russo-Japanese War that had since served in the Japanese Navy under the name Iki as a coastal defense ship. In April 1916, she patrolled the Chinese coast with her newly launched sisters Kirishima and Haruna. From 1917 to the end of World War I, Hiei stayed mostly at Sasebo, patrolling the Chinese and Korean coasts with her sister ships on several occasions.

After World War I ended, Japan gained control of former German possessions in the central Pacific under the Treaty of Versailles. Because of Japan's warm relations with Britain and the United States at the time, Hiei and other Japanese warships became much less active. Other than a patrol alongside Haruna and Kirishima off the Chinese coast in March 1919, Hiei stayed in Japanese home ports. On 13 October 1920, she was placed in reserve. Following the Great Kantō earthquake of September 1923, the Japanese Navy's capital ships helped with rescue work until the end of the month. Hiei arrived at Kure Naval Base on 1 December 1923 for a refit that increased the elevation of her main guns from 20 to 33 degrees and rebuilt her foremast.

With the end of World War I, the world powers tried to curb any militarization that might spark another war. Under the 1922 Washington Naval Treaty, the Imperial Japanese Navy was significantly cut back, with a 5:5:3 ratio required between the capital ships of Britain, the United States, and Japan. The treaty also banned Japan from building new capital ships until 1931, and capped any capital ship at 35,000 tons. Existing capital ships could still be upgraded with improved torpedo bulges and deck armor, as long as the additions didn't exceed 3,000 tons. By the time the Washington Treaty was fully in effect in Japan, only three classes of World War I-era capital ships remained active: the Fusō-class and Ise-class battleships, and the Kongō-class battlecruisers.

In July 1927, Crown Prince Takamatsu, Emperor Hirohito's younger brother, was assigned to Hiei. From October to November 1927, the ship underwent a minor refit at Sasebo to carry two Yokosuka E1Y floatplanes, though no launch catapults were added. On 29 March 1928, Hiei left Sasebo alongside Kongō and the battleships Nagato and Fusō to patrol off the Chusan Archipelago, then arrived alongside Kongō at Port Arthur in April 1928. In October 1929, she returned to Kure to prepare for demilitarization and reconstruction.

1929–1937: Demilitarization and use as a Training Ship

To avoid scrapping Hiei under the Washington Treaty, the Imperial Japanese Navy decided to convert her into a demilitarized training ship. On 15 October 1929, she went into drydock at Kure Naval Arsenal. Her No. 4 gun turret was removed, and she was stripped of all eight submerged torpedo tubes, her 6-inch guns, and her armor belt. All but nine of her boilers were removed, cutting her speed to 18 knots (33 km/h), and one of her three funnels was taken out. She was reclassified as a reserve ship at the end of November 1929. On 24 April 1930, reconstruction was halted after the signing of the London Naval Treaty, which further restricted battleship construction among the major naval powers, and preservation work began at Sasebo instead. Reconstruction didn't resume until July 1931.

In September 1931, Japanese army units invaded the Chinese province of Manchuria, turning it into the puppet state of Manchukuo. In December 1932, Hiei was reassigned to the Navy's training squadron. On 25 February 1933, the League of Nations ruled that Japan had violated Chinese sovereignty and international law with the invasion. Refusing to accept the ruling, Japan withdrew from the League that same day, also pulling out of the Washington and London Naval Treaties, which freed the Imperial Japanese Navy from all restrictions on capital ship construction. From late May to 13 August 1933, Hiei received upgrades allowing her to perform regular duties for the Emperor, and she served as his observation ship for the Imperial Naval Review three days later. From January to March 1934, her No. 4 turret and ammunition magazine were refitted. In November 1935, Hiei served as the Emperor's ship for his official visit to the Kagoshima and Miyazaki prefectures.

1937–1941: Reconstruction and Service as a Fast Battleship

No longer bound by treaty limits, the Imperial Japanese Navy reconstructed Hiei along the same lines as her sisters. She received eight new oil-fired Kampon boilers and geared turbines, while her stern was lengthened by 26 feet (7.9 m) to increase speed. Her aft 14-inch turret was refitted and fire control systems installed for all four main turrets. The elevation of her main and secondary guns was increased, and she was equipped with two Nakajima E8N and Kawanishi E7K reconnaissance floatplanes, with catapults and launch rails fitted aft of turret No. 3. Fourteen of her 6-inch guns were refitted, and an anti-aircraft suite of eight 5-inch dual-purpose guns and ten twin mounts of Type 96 25 mm autocannons was added. Her superstructure was rebuilt as a prototype of the tower-mast design later used on the Yamato-class battleships, then still being designed.

Hiei's armor was also extensively upgraded. Her main belt was reapplied and strengthened to a uniform 8 inches (previously varying from 6 to 8 inches), with diagonal bulkheads 5 to 8 inches (127 to 203 mm) deep reinforcing the main belt. Turret armor was strengthened to 10 inches (254 mm), and 4 inches (102 mm) were added to parts of the deck armor. The armor around her ammunition magazines was also strengthened during the refit. Though still less heavily armored than other Japanese battleships, Hiei was now significantly faster. The reconstruction was declared complete on 31 January 1940. Capable of up to 30.5 knots (56.5 km/h), Hiei was reclassified as a fast battleship. She took part in the Imperial Fleet Review in October 1940, inspected by Emperor Hirohito, members of the royal family, Navy Minister Koshirō Oikawa, and Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto. In November, she was assigned to the Third Battleship Division of the First Fleet.

On 26 November 1941, Hiei left Hitokappu Bay in the Kurile Islands alongside Kirishima and six Japanese fast carriers of the First Air Fleet Striking Force (Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, Hiryū, Shōkaku, and Zuikaku) under Vice-Admiral Chuichi Nagumo. On 7 December 1941, aircraft from these six carriers attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, sinking four US Navy battleships and many other vessels. Following the attack and the US declaration of war, Hiei returned to Japan.

1942: Combat and loss

On 17 January 1942, Hiei left Truk Lagoon Naval Base with the Third Battleship Division to support carrier operations against Rabaul and Kavieng. In February, she deployed with a force of carriers and destroyers in response to American raids on Japanese bases in the Gilbert and Marshall Islands. On 1 March, following carrier operations against Darwin and Java in the Dutch East Indies, Hiei, Kirishima, and Chikuma, acting as escorts for the carrier task force, engaged the destroyer USS Edsall, with Hiei firing 210 14-inch and seventy 6-inch shells. When the ships failed to score any hits, dive-bombers from three of Admiral Nagumo's carriers immobilized the destroyer, which was then finished off by gunfire from the three ships.

In April 1942, Hiei and the Third Battleship Division joined five fleet carriers and two cruisers in a large raid against British naval forces in the Indian Ocean. On 5 April, Easter Sunday, the Japanese fleet attacked the harbor at Colombo, Ceylon, while seaplanes from the cruiser Tone spotted two fleeing British cruisers, both later sunk by air attack. On 8 April, Japanese carrier aircraft attacked the Royal Navy base at Trincomalee, only to find Admiral James Somerville's British Eastern Fleet had already withdrawn. Returning from the attack, a floatplane from Hiei's sister ship Haruna spotted the aircraft carrier HMS Hermes, which was quickly sunk by a massive air attack. The fleet then returned to Japan, arriving home on 23 April.

On 27 May 1942, Hiei sailed with Kongō and the heavy cruisers Atago, Chōkai, Myōkō, and Haguro as part of Admiral Nobutake Kondō's Invasion Force during the Battle of Midway. After the Combined Fleet lost four fast carriers on 4 June, Kondō's force withdrew to Japan. In July, Hiei was drydocked for refits to her aircraft complement and the addition of single and twin 25 mm gun mounts. In August, she escorted the carrier Shōkaku during the Battle of the Eastern Solomons. In October, Hiei sailed as part of Rear Admiral Abe's Vanguard Force, providing distant cover as Kongō and Haruna nearly destroyed Henderson Field on Guadalcanal on the night of 13 October. From 26 to 30 October, Hiei and her sisters took part in the Battle of the Santa Cruz Islands.

Naval Battle of Guadalcanal

On 10 November 1942, Hiei left Truk with Kirishima, the light cruiser Nagara, and eleven destroyers under Vice Admiral Hiroaki Abe, aiming to shell American positions near Henderson Field ahead of a major troop convoy. US reconnaissance spotted the force days in advance, and Rear Admiral Daniel Callaghan brought two heavy cruisers, three light cruisers, and eight destroyers to meet them in Ironbottom Sound. At 01:24 on 13 November, USS Helena detected the Japanese force 28,000 yards out. Because Abe hadn't expected resistance, his battleships' main guns were loaded with anti-aircraft shells meant for bombarding Henderson Field, which limited the damage their opening salvos could do.

t 01:50, Hiei lit up the light cruiser USS Atlanta with her searchlights; between Hiei's and Nagara's gunfire and a torpedo from the destroyer Ikazuchi, Atlanta was left crippled and later sank with 170 men lost. American destroyers closed to point-blank range: USS Cushing missed with torpedoes and was sunk by Nagara and escorting destroyers; USS O'Bannon hit Hiei with 5-inch shells and dud torpedoes, and USS Laffey nearly collided with her before raking her superstructure with shell fire, wounding Admiral Abe and killing his staff officer.

Unable to depress her main guns low enough to hit the nearby destroyers, Hiei turned her fire on American cruisers instead. Together with Kirishima, she crippled the flagship USS San Francisco with twelve 14-inch hits, killing Admiral Callaghan, but San Francisco's return fire hit Hiei's steering gear room, jamming her rudder and leaving her unable to maneuver. Hiei also traded hits with USS Portland, and once the American destroyers came back into range, hit Laffey with two 14-inch shells (she was ultimately sunk by a torpedo from Yukikaze) and helped finish off USS Monssen, already crippled by Japanese destroyers.

n total, Hiei took at least 85 shell hits and up to nine torpedo hits, most of which failed to detonate. With his flagship crippled, Abe ordered the rest of the fleet to withdraw at 02:00. Kirishima tried to tow her, but flooding jammed her rudder to starboard, forcing her to steer in circles. Through the morning, Hiei was hit repeatedly by B-17 bombers and carrier aircraft from USS Enterprise, taking a combined 5-8 torpedo hits and several bomb hits on top of the damage from the night battle. Her crew abandoned ship, and escorting destroyers scuttled her with torpedoes. Hiei sank on the evening of 13 November 1942 with the loss of 188 crew, the first Japanese battleship lost in World War II. Her wreck was found in 2019 lying upside down near Savo Island, her bow gone forward of the bridge from a magazine explosion.

Discovery of Hiei's Wreck

On 6 February 2019, Paul Allen's exploration ship RV Petrel announced the discovery of Hiei's wreck. According to Petrel, the main body of Hiei now lies upside down in 3,000 feet (900 m) of water northwest of Savo Island in the Solomon Islands. As with her sister ship Kirishima, the bow is gone forward of the bridge due to a magazine explosion.
